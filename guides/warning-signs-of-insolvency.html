The warning signs of insolvency

Insolvency rarely arrives out of nowhere. Australian courts have a well-used checklist of the signs that come first. Here’s what they are, and what they look like in your books.

8 min readLast reviewed October 2026General information, not advice

On this page

What insolvency means

Under the Corporations Act, a company is solvent if it can pay all its debts as and when they become due. If it can’t, it’s insolvent.

That makes insolvency a cash-flow test, not a balance-sheet one. A business can own valuable equipment and property and still be insolvent if it can’t pay this month’s bills on time. Equally, a business with negative net assets might be solvent for now if its lenders keep supporting it.

Why directors should care

Directors have a duty to prevent the company trading while insolvent. If a company incurs a debt when there are reasonable grounds to suspect it’s insolvent, directors can be held personally liable for that debt.

The law gives directors protections and options, such as safe harbour and small business restructuring, but most of them work best, or only work, when they start early. Recognising the warning signs is what makes early action possible.

The 14 warning signs

In ASIC v Plymin (2003), the Supreme Court of Victoria listed indicators that a company may be insolvent. Courts, regulators and practitioners still refer to them. No single sign proves insolvency; it’s the overall picture that counts.

  1. Continuing losses
  2. Liquidity ratios below 1
  3. Overdue Commonwealth and State taxes
  4. A poor relationship with the current bank, including being unable to borrow more
  5. No access to other finance
  6. Being unable to raise more equity capital
  7. Suppliers putting the company on cash-on-delivery terms, or demanding special payments before supplying
  8. Creditors unpaid outside their trading terms
  9. Issuing post-dated cheques
  10. Dishonoured cheques
  11. Special arrangements with selected creditors
  12. Solicitors’ letters, summonses, judgments or warrants issued against the company
  13. Payments to creditors in rounded sums that don’t match specific invoices
  14. Being unable to produce timely and accurate financial information

If you recognise the signs

  • Don’t wait for the year-end accounts. The options narrow as the months pass.
  • Get the facts in one place: cash position, what’s owed to the ATO, suppliers and the bank, and what customers owe you.
  • Talk to your accountant early, and if the signs are serious, to a registered liquidator or restructuring adviser. A first conversation is often free and is confidential, and it doesn’t commit you to anything.
  • Keep lodging on time. Up-to-date tax lodgements and paid employee entitlements are conditions of safe harbour and small business restructuring.
