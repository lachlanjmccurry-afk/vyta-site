Safe harbour and restructuring: the options for directors

If your company is struggling, closing it isn’t the only option. Australian law offers several paths, and the earlier you start, the more of them stay open.

7 min readLast reviewed October 2026General information, not advice

On this page

Insolvent trading Safe harbour Small business restructuring Voluntary administration Liquidation Which path fits?

Insolvent trading

Directors must not let a company take on new debts when there are reasonable grounds to suspect it’s insolvent. If they do, they can be personally liable for those debts, and face civil penalties.

That duty is why timing matters so much. Once the warning signs are there, carrying on as usual is risky for the directors personally. The options below are the main ways to act.

Safe harbour

Safe harbour protects directors from personal liability for insolvent trading while they develop and carry out a plan to turn the company around, as long as the plan is reasonably likely to lead to a better outcome than going straight into administration or liquidation.

What it usually involves

  • Recognising the problem early and getting advice from an appropriately qualified adviser
  • Keeping proper financial records
  • Developing a realistic restructuring plan, and acting on it

Conditions to keep in mind

  • Employee entitlements, including superannuation, must be paid when due.
  • Tax lodgements must be kept up to date.

Safe harbour isn’t an application or a formal appointment. It depends on what the directors actually do, which is why working with an adviser and keeping a record of decisions matters.

Small business restructuring

Small business restructuring (SBR) is a formal process designed for smaller companies. It lets the business keep trading under the directors’ control while a plan to pay creditors is put together.

  • Eligibility: broadly, companies with total liabilities under $1 million. A practitioner can confirm whether yours qualifies.
  • Who’s involved: a small business restructuring practitioner, who helps prepare the plan and oversees it.
  • Timing: the plan is usually developed within 20 business days, and then creditors vote on it.
  • Before it can proceed: employee entitlements that are due must be paid, and tax lodgements must be up to date.

Voluntary administration

In voluntary administration, the directors appoint an independent administrator, who takes control of the company. The administrator investigates its affairs and creditors then decide its future: often a deed of company arrangement that lets the business continue, or liquidation.

Administration suits larger or more complex situations, or cases where the business needs breathing space from creditors while a deal is put together.

Liquidation

When a business can’t be saved, a creditors’ voluntary liquidation lets the directors and shareholders close it in an orderly way. A registered liquidator sells the assets, investigates the company’s affairs and pays creditors as far as the money allows. Sometimes the business, or part of it, can be sold as a going concern.

Choosing an orderly exit is often far better for directors than waiting for a creditor to wind the company up.

Which path fits?

That’s a question for a qualified adviser, but two questions do most of the sorting, and they’re the two Vyta scores:

  • How serious is the financial trouble? (Distress)
  • Would the business make money if the cash problem were fixed? (Viability)

A business in serious trouble that still makes money underneath, which Vyta calls a restructure candidate, is the kind of business safe harbour, SBR and administration are designed for. A business that doesn’t make money underneath usually needs a different conversation.
